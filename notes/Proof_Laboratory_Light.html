START HERE

Read the problem before choosing a theorem

Mathematics begins with a precise task. A familiar-looking question can hide a new condition.

01GIVEN

What objects are allowed? Look for positive, integer, distinct, for every, on a segment, on a line, adjacent, and nonzero.

02TARGET

What must the last line establish: an existence claim, the exact maximum, an impossibility, all solutions, or equality?

03GAP

What has already been shown, and what is only suspected? Describe what a counterexample would look like.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Finding answers is not the same as verifying them

Find all real numbers x such that √(x + 2) = x.

  1. Domain: The square root is nonnegative, so any solution has x ≥ 0.
  2. Candidate step: Squaring gives x + 2 = x², hence (x − 2)(x + 1) = 0. The candidates are x = 2 and x = −1.
  3. Check the original: x = 2 gives √4 = 2. The candidate −1 is invalid, since √1 ≠ −1.

The only solution is x = 2. Squaring supplied necessary candidates; checking finished the job.

STUDY NOTE

The proof finish line

The most important difference is between seeing a pattern and showing why it must be true.

A good idea is the beginning of a proof, not the end. Before starting, identify what the question is asking you to establish. Most olympiad problems require more than a numerical answer.

01Claim

Record the permitted objects and the exact target. For a maximum or minimum, a bound and an attainable example have different jobs. For a classification, show all solutions have the proposed form and check the surviving candidates.

02Reason

Link your observations by justified steps. No unexplained leaps.

03Close

State exactly why the conclusion follows. Check every case.

Four different proof tasks

A construction shows one example can work. A universal bound shows every allowed example obeys a limit. A necessary condition holds for all actual solutions but may allow extras; a sufficient condition guarantees all original requirements. Classifications need both directions or justified equivalences.

WORKED MODEL

A complete existence and optimality proof

  1. Problem: two nonnegative real numbers have sum 12. What is the largest possible product?
  2. Upper bound: from (a − b)² ≥ 0, we have (a + b)² ≥ 4ab.
  3. As a + b = 12, we obtain 144 ≥ 4ab and therefore ab ≤ 36.
  4. Construction: choose a = b = 6. Then ab = 36.

Finish the proof: The product cannot exceed 36, and the choice (6, 6) achieves 36. Hence the maximum is exactly 36.

Closing checklist

What have I proved? Does the proof cover every allowed case? If I guessed a formula, have I proved both that no other answers exist and that mine work? If I claim a maximum, have I given both the bound and a construction?

PRACTICAL TOOLKIT

Eight methods to reach for first

A technique is a move. Choosing it for a reason is a method. Explaining every necessary step is a proof.

Do not memorise thirty theorem names today. Learn the eight common moves below. Each card tells you what job it can finish and what it cannot do.

01Geometry correspondenceOpen method

The move: List the vertices matched in similar triangles and justify each angle or ratio.

When to try: Triangles, parallel segments, circles, and equal-angle configurations.

Example: In triangles ADE and ABC, correspondence A to A, D to B, E to C gives DE/BC = AD/AB.

Warning: A diagram or an incorrect correspondence cannot establish similarity.

Finish-line check: State which vertices match and which theorem proves the relation.

02Candidates and verificationOpen method

The move: Derive possible answers, then test each against the original conditions.

When to try: Find all; an equation was squared; only special cases of a functional equation were used.

Example: Squaring √(x + 2) = x gives x = 2 or −1, but only 2 passes the original equation.

Warning: Necessary conditions are not automatically sufficient.

Finish-line check: Both directions or a valid equivalence appear in the proof.

03Factorisation and squaresOpen method

The move: Turn a target difference into factors with known signs.

When to try: An inequality is tight when variables coincide; polynomial expressions recur.

Example: For real a, b, (a − b)² ≥ 0 implies a² + b² ≥ 2ab.

Warning: A product with an uncontrolled sign is not known to be nonnegative.

Finish-line check: Show the identity, each sign, and equality cases.

04InductionOpen method

The move: Prove a base and a valid n to n+1 step, preserving every coefficient.

When to try: Statements indexed by integers with a usable recurrence.

Example: For n ≥ 1, the sum of the first n odd integers is n²: prove the base and add 2n + 1.

Warning: Spotting a pattern is not an induction hypothesis or step.

Finish-line check: Check initial values, step algebra and the full integer range.

05Residues and parityOpen method

The move: Classify possible remainders to exclude impossible equations.

When to try: Divisibility, squares, cubes, integer solutions.

Example: A square modulo 4 is 0 or 1, so x² + y² cannot equal 4z + 3.

Warning: Checking some examples is not an exhaustive residue proof.

Finish-line check: List every input residue and explain its output.

06Bound and constructionOpen method

The move: Show a universal bound and exhibit an allowed example attaining it.

When to try: Maximum, minimum or least possible value.

Example: If every configuration allows at most 5 items and one valid configuration has 5, the maximum is 5.

Warning: One construction never establishes a universal optimum.

Finish-line check: Prove every candidate obeys the bound and one attains it.

07PigeonholeOpen method

The move: Assign objects to categories and force a shared category.

When to try: More objects than possible labels or residues.

Example: Among 6 integers, two share a remainder modulo 5; their difference is divisible by 5.

Warning: A shared category might not imply the desired property.

Finish-line check: Check category coverage, count and the forced relationship.

08InvariantsOpen method

The move: Find a quantity preserved by every legal operation.

When to try: Repeated moves, reachability, final state independent of order.

Example: Flipping exactly two lamps preserves parity of the number switched on.

Warning: A necessary invariant match does not prove reachability.

Finish-line check: Prove preservation for all moves and state exactly what follows.

STUDY NOTE

Algebra

Make each transformation lawful. Separate possible answers from verified answers.

In algebra, the crucial habit is to make each line follow from the last. A correct identity can be useful without yet proving the target statement.

Check the direction of your reasoning

(a − b)² ≥ 0
⇒ a² + b² ≥ 2ab

This establishes the inequality for every real a and b. Equality occurs precisely when a = b.

Two tests before writing the next line

Degree: under a scaling a → ta, b → tb, a homogeneous degree-3 expression scales by t³. If an identity is homogeneous, scaling all variables by t multiplies every term of degree 3 by t³. Degree matching catches some mistakes, but not incorrect coefficients. Mixed degrees can be legitimate when a given constraint or substitution explains them. Equivalence: if you square, divide or cancel an expression, explain why the step is valid, especially if a term might be zero or negative.

WORKED MODEL

Solving a functional equation on the integers

  1. Suppose g : ℤ → ℤ satisfies g(x + y) = g(x) + g(y) for all integers x, y. Find every such function.
  2. Putting x = y = 0 gives g(0) = g(0) + g(0), hence g(0) = 0.
  3. Let c = g(1). Setting y = 1 gives g(n + 1) = g(n) + c. At n = 0, g(0) = 0 = c·0. If g(n) = cn, the recurrence gives g(n + 1) = cn + c = c(n + 1). Thus induction proves g(n) = cn for all nonnegative integers n.
  4. Putting y = −x gives g(−x) = −g(x). Hence g(n) = cn for negative integers too.
  5. Conversely, for any integer c define g(n) = cn. For every integer pair x, y, g(x + y) = c(x + y) = cx + cy = g(x) + g(y). Thus all such candidates work, so this is the complete classification.

Finish the proof: Every solution has the form g(n) = cn with c ∈ ℤ, and every one of these functions works.

When using induction

State the starting value, the induction hypothesis and the exact statement for the next value. In a recurrence, copy coefficients carefully. For example, an equation with 2f(1) must not silently become f(1).

METHOD SELECTION

AM-GM, Schur, and the direction of a bound

A true inequality can still be insufficient. The question is not only whether a tool is valid, but whether its conclusion moves the proof towards the target.

WORKED EXAMPLE

First diagnose the proposed route

For positive a, b, c, prove

(a + b + c)³ + 9abc ≥ 4(a + b + c)(ab + bc + ca).

Write p = a + b + c, q = ab + bc + ca and r = abc. The target becomes p³ + 9r ≥ 4pq.

AM-GM correctly gives p³ ≥ 27r, and therefore p³ + 9r ≥ 36r. But to conclude the target via this route we would also need 36r ≥ 4pq. At (a, b, c) = (1, 1, 2), the bound gives 72 while the target right side is 80. So that bridge does not follow.

What went wrong? This particular AM-GM step provides too weak a lower bound for the entire left side. It does not show that AM-GM is incapable of proving the inequality.

PROOF 1

Expand, order, and factor

Set D equal to the left side minus the right side. Direct expansion gives

D = a³ + b³ + c³ + 3abc − (a²b + a²c + b²a + b²c + c²a + c²b).

This is symmetric, so relabel a ≥ b ≥ c > 0. The identity below follows by expansion, not guesswork:

D = (a − b)²(a + b − c) + c(a − c)(b − c).

Both terms are nonnegative: the square is nonnegative; a + b − c is positive; and c, a − c, b − c are nonnegative. Thus D ≥ 0.

If D = 0, the first term forces a = b, and the second then forces a = c since c > 0. Conversely all-equal variables give equality. Hence equality holds exactly when a = b = c.

Proof 2: AM-GM does work, if you choose better quantitiesOptional advanced proof

Another checked identity is D = abc − (b + c − a)(c + a − b)(a + b − c). Put x = b + c − a, y = c + a − b, z = a + b − c. Then x + y = 2c, y + z = 2a, and z + x = 2b are all positive.

At most one of x, y, z can be nonpositive. If one is, xyz ≤ 0 < abc. Otherwise all three are positive, and AM-GM gives xy ≤ c², yz ≤ a² and zx ≤ b². Multiply to get (xyz)² ≤ (abc)², so xyz ≤ abc. Therefore D ≥ 0.

Equality in the positive case requires x = y = z and hence a = b = c. This proves exactly the same target with AM-GM. The difference is the choice of quantities.

Schur's inequality

The inequality is often called Schur's cubic inequality. The name is useful later, but it is not a prerequisite for understanding either proof. For a first encounter, explore an expansion, the equality case, and one hint about factoring.

Direction checklist: What quantity is bounded? Is the bound an upper or lower bound? Is that the direction your target needs? Do the equality cases fit? If the proof uses division, do you know the sign?
PROOF COMPLETION

What induction proves, and what checking proves

Induction can establish a candidate family perfectly. A complete classification must also ensure every retained candidate satisfies every original condition.

WORKED EXAMPLE

When an extra condition eliminates candidates

Find all functions g : ℤ → ℤ satisfying, for every pair of integers x, y, both

g(x + y) = g(x) + g(y)   and   g(xy) = g(x)g(y).
  1. Find candidates: Additivity forces g(0) = 0. With c = g(1), the recurrence g(n + 1) = g(n) + c and induction give g(n) = cn for n ≥ 0. Since g(−n) = −g(n), this extends to all integers.
  2. Check the other requirement: The multiplicative equation at x = y = 1 gives c = c². Therefore c = 0 or c = 1. In particular the additive candidate g(n) = 2n fails.
  3. Verify the survivors: The zero function and identity function both satisfy additivity and multiplicativity for every integer pair.

Thus exactly g(n) = 0 and g(n) = n work. A general recurrence alone did not decide which constants survive the second condition.

For the original quadratic equation f(x+y) + f(x−y) = 2f(x) + 2f(y), the proposed family f(n) = cn² passes the universal check because

c(x+y)² + c(x−y)² = 2cx² + 2cy².

By contrast, f(n) = cn² + k gives 2cx² + 2cy² + 2k on the left but 2cx² + 2cy² + 4k on the right. It satisfies the equation only when k = 0.

Nuance:

If every step of your proof is already reversible, the final check may be built into that argument. What matters is proving both logical directions, not mechanically repeating a ritual.

STUDY NOTE

Geometry

Use the diagram to discover; use named relationships to prove.

A diagram is a place to discover relationships. Your written proof must then explain why those relationships hold. A picture that looks convincing is not a justification on its own.

A labelled diagram helps to map equal ratios and corresponding angles.

WORKED MODEL

A midpoint proof

  1. Let D and E be the midpoints of AB and AC respectively. We want to show DE ∥ BC and DE = BC/2.
  2. By the midpoint conditions, AD/AB = AE/AC = 1/2.
  3. The included angle ∠DAE equals ∠BAC because D lies on AB and E lies on AC.
  4. Therefore triangles ADE and ABC are similar by the side-angle-side similarity criterion.
  5. Corresponding angles are equal, giving ∠ADE = ∠ABC; hence DE ∥ BC. Also DE/BC = AD/AB = 1/2.

Finish the proof: Both the parallelism and the length relation follow from the same justified similarity statement.

Geometry proof order

State which points are collinear or perpendicular. Name the theorem you use: corresponding angles, cyclic quadrilaterals, similarity, or the tangent-chord theorem. State the vertex correspondence before using similarity. For triangles ADE and ABC it is A to A, D to B, E to C, so DE corresponds to BC. The labels must match the proven angles and side ratios. If feet lie on extensions, ordinary angle arguments may need directed angles.

STUDY NOTE

Number theory

Residues, exact divisibility and the logic of infinite descent.

Trying small values is excellent for discovery. To finish, explain why the pattern must hold for all relevant integers.

Modular arithmetic as a filter

Modulo 5, the inputs 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 have square residues 0, 1, 4, 4, 1 respectively. This complete mapping explains which residue can yield a square divisible by 5. This gives a short proof of an important fact.

WORKED MODEL

A divisibility proof

  1. Suppose 5 divides n².
  2. Any integer is congruent to 0, 1, 2, 3 or 4 modulo 5. The corresponding square residues are 0, 1, 4, 4, 1, in that order.
  3. The only way n² can be congruent to 0 modulo 5 is when n ≡ 0 (mod 5).

Finish the proof: Thus 5 divides n. The argument covers every integer, not just tested examples.

Infinite descent

Descent is a contradiction strategy. Suppose a nonzero integer solution exists. Choose one with a positive integer measure as small as possible. Show that its existence would force another solution with a strictly smaller positive measure. This is impossible.

WORKED MODEL

A miniature descent

  1. Assume x² = 2y² has a nonzero integer solution. If y = 0, the equation gives x = 0, contrary to the nonzero assumption. So y ≠ 0, and among nonzero solutions choose one with the smallest positive |y|.
  2. x² is even, so x is even. Write x = 2u. Then y² = 2u², so y is also even; write y = 2v.
  3. Substitution gives u² = 2v², a new integer solution.
  4. But 0 < |v| = |y|/2 < |y|, contradicting minimality.

Finish the proof: There is no nonzero integer solution to x² = 2y².

Exact divisibility versus divisibility

For nonzero N, to show v₃(N) = r you need both 3ʳ | N and 3ʳ⁺¹ ∤ N. Showing only that 3ʳ divides N gives a lower bound, not the exact exponent.

STUDY NOTE

Combinatorics

Find what is preserved, what is unavoidable and what makes a bound sharp.

Combinatorics frequently rewards finding a quantity that stays unchanged, a property that must always hold, or a construction showing a bound is sharp.

Find an invariant

If a process repeatedly changes a configuration, ask: what expression could stay the same after every legal move?

WORKED MODEL

A multiplicative invariant

  1. Start with the entries 1, 2, 3. A move replaces any two entries a, b with a + b + 2ab.
  2. Observe that 1 + 2(a + b + 2ab) = (1 + 2a)(1 + 2b).
  3. Therefore the product of all factors (1 + 2 × entry) does not change after a move.
  4. Initially it is 3 × 5 × 7 = 105. If the last entry is T, then 1 + 2T = 105.

Finish the proof: T = 52, regardless of which legal pair is combined first.

Universal claims need universal arguments

An example proves that a configuration can exist. To prove that every configuration has a feature, you need a theorem or argument that covers all configurations, not just the one drawn on your page.

WORKED MODEL

Sharpness using the pigeonhole principle

  1. Problem: find the least N such that any N integers contain two whose difference is divisible by 5.
  2. Upper bound: there are only five possible remainders modulo 5. Among six integers, two share a remainder.
  3. Subtracting those two integers gives a multiple of 5.
  4. Counterexample for smaller N: the five integers 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 all have distinct remainders.

Finish the proof: Six works for every choice; five can fail. Therefore the least N is 6.

STUDY NOTE

Your practice laboratory

Short exercises with optional hints and model proofs.

These are guided learning questions, not scored examination items. Attempt just two or three in a sitting. Each has a hint and a complete model argument you can open when you are ready. Try to give a reason for every step.

01
ALGEBRA

Prove that 1 + 3 + 5 + ··· + (2n − 1) = n² for every integer n ≥ 1.

Focus: Use every part of induction.

Show first hint

Show the claim for n = 1, then add 2n + 1 to both sides of the induction hypothesis.

Show model proof

At n = 1 both sides equal 1. Assume the first n odd numbers sum to n². The first n + 1 sum to n² + (2n + 1) = (n + 1)². Thus the formula holds for all n ≥ 1 by induction.

02
ALGEBRA

For real numbers x and y, prove x² + y² ≥ 2xy and give the equality condition.

Focus: Give both the bound and equality case.

Show first hint

Think of a square that cannot be negative.

Show model proof

(x − y)² ≥ 0. Expanding yields x² + y² − 2xy ≥ 0. Equality holds if and only if x − y = 0, meaning x = y.

03
ALGEBRA

Let h : ℤ → ℤ satisfy h(x + y) = h(x) + h(y) for all integers x and y. Prove h(−n) = −h(n) for every integer n.

Focus: Use the original quantifier correctly.

Show first hint

Substitute y = −x. First find h(0).

Show model proof

Putting x = y = 0 gives h(0) = 2h(0), so h(0) = 0. Then h(n) + h(−n) = h(0) = 0. Thus h(−n) = −h(n) for every integer n.

04
GEOMETRY

In triangle ABC, D and E are the midpoints of AB and AC. Prove DE = BC/2.

Focus: Name the triangles and prove similarity.

Show first hint

Which two triangles have a shared angle and matching side ratios?

Show model proof

AD/AB = AE/AC = 1/2 and ∠DAE = ∠BAC. Therefore triangles ADE and ABC are similar by SAS. Matching sides give DE/BC = 1/2. Thus DE = BC/2.

05
GEOMETRY

A triangle has interior angles α, β and γ. Prove α + β + γ = 180°.

Focus: Do not use the picture alone as justification.

Show first hint

Through one vertex, draw a line parallel to the opposite side.

Show model proof

Through A draw a line ℓ parallel to BC. The angles formed between ℓ and AB, and between AC and ℓ, equal ∠ABC and ∠BCA by alternate interior angles. These two angles, together with ∠BAC, lie adjacent along a straight line at A. Their sum is 180°, so ∠ABC + ∠BAC + ∠BCA = 180°.

06
NUMBER THEORY

Prove that if 5 divides n², then 5 divides n.

Focus: Explain why the modular possibilities are exhaustive.

Show first hint

List square residues modulo 5.

Show model proof

For n ≡ 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 (mod 5), the values n² are congruent to 0, 1, 4, 4, 1. Only n ≡ 0 gives n² ≡ 0, hence 5 | n.

07
NUMBER THEORY

Show that 7 divides 8ⁿ − 1 for every integer n ≥ 1.

Focus: Move from examples to all n.

Show first hint

Reduce 8 modulo 7.

Show model proof

Since 8 ≡ 1 (mod 7), raising both sides to the positive integer power n gives 8ⁿ ≡ 1ⁿ ≡ 1 (mod 7). Hence 7 | (8ⁿ − 1) for every n ≥ 1.

08
NUMBER THEORY

Explain why a proof of v₃(N) = 2 needs more than proving 9 divides N. Give an example.

Focus: Distinguish exact from at least.

Show first hint

What would disprove exactness?

Show model proof

The exact equality v₃(N) = 2 means 9 | N and 27 ∤ N. For example, 27 is divisible by 9 but v₃(27) = 3, not 2. Hence divisibility by 9 alone is insufficient.

09
COMBINATORICS

Six integers are chosen. Prove that two have a difference divisible by 5.

Focus: Make the universal argument explicit.

Show first hint

How many remainder classes are available?

Show model proof

There are five possible residues modulo 5. The pigeonhole principle forces two of six integers to share a residue. Their difference is congruent to 0 modulo 5 and hence is divisible by 5.

10
COMBINATORICS

Starting with 2, 3, 4, repeatedly replace any pair a, b by a + b + 2ab. Prove that the final number is 157 regardless of move order.

Focus: Specify the invariant and why it is preserved.

Show first hint

Look at 1 + 2(a + b + 2ab).

Show model proof

Since 1 + 2(a + b + 2ab) = (1 + 2a)(1 + 2b), the product of (1 + 2 × entry) over all entries is unchanged by every move. Initially it equals 5 × 7 × 9 = 315. If the last entry is T, then 1 + 2T = 315 and T = 157. The algebra applies to every legal pair, so the order does not matter.

11
PROOF STRUCTURE

A student finds an example reaching a claimed maximum. What additional step is needed to prove it really is the maximum?

Focus: Tell existence apart from optimality.

Show first hint

An example establishes only one direction.

Show model proof

The example establishes attainability. A separate universal upper bound must show no allowed configuration can exceed the claimed value. Once the bound and an attaining example agree, maximality follows.

12
PROOF STRUCTURE

Let g : ℤ → ℤ satisfy both g(x + y) = g(x) + g(y) and g(xy) = g(x)g(y) for all integers x, y. The first condition forces g(n) = cn for some integer c. Determine which c work and prove the complete answer.

Focus: Separate candidate discovery from a full list of solutions.

Show first hint

Use x = y = 1 in the multiplicative condition. Then test the remaining candidates in both original conditions.

Show model proof

Additivity gives g(n) = cn for some integer c. At x = y = 1, multiplicativity gives c = c², so c = 0 or c = 1. If c = 0, g is the zero function and both equations hold for every integer pair. If c = 1, g(n) = n and both also hold. No other c survives. This shows why a candidate list derived from one condition can be too broad.

UNSEEN-STYLE PRACTICE

Transfer laboratory

Six new exercises. Their titles do not tell you exactly which move will finish them.

Start by writing Given, Target, Already forced, and Still to prove. These are learning exercises with optional hints, not an official BMO paper. If you open a hint, keep that attempt classified as guided practice.

F1
ALGEBRA / INEQUALITY

For real t > 0, prove t + 1/t ≥ 2, with equality only when t = 1. What changes for t < 0?

Transfer exercise: choose your own tool before checking the hint.

Show first hint

Start with (t − 1)² ≥ 0. Think about the sign when dividing.

Show model proof

From (t − 1)² ≥ 0, we have t² + 1 ≥ 2t. For t > 0, division gives t + 1/t ≥ 2, equality only at t = 1. For t < 0, the positive-case inequality must not be applied directly. Applying it to −t > 0 gives −t − 1/t ≥ 2, hence t + 1/t ≤ −2, with equality at t = −1.

F2
NUMBER THEORY / RESIDUES

Prove that no integers x, y satisfy x² + y² ≡ 3 (mod 4).

Transfer exercise: choose your own tool before checking the hint.

Show first hint

Write an even or odd integer, square it, and cover both possibilities.

Show model proof

For n = 2k, n² ≡ 0 (mod 4); for n = 2k + 1, n² = 4k(k + 1) + 1 ≡ 1 (mod 4). Thus x² + y² has remainder 0, 1, or 2, never 3.

I1
ALGEBRA / FUNCTIONAL EQUATIONS

Find all f : ℤ → ℤ with f(x + y) + f(x − y) = 2f(x) for all integers x, y.

Transfer exercise: choose your own tool before checking the hint.

Show first hint

Set y = 1 to obtain a recurrence. Be alert to a constant term.

Show model proof

Let b = f(0) and a = f(1) − f(0). Using y = 1, f(x + 1) − f(x) = f(x) − f(x − 1), so successive first differences agree. Induction forward and backward shows f(n) = an + b for every integer n. Conversely, f(x+y) + f(x−y) = (ax+ay+b) + (ax−ay+b) = 2ax + 2b = 2f(x). Hence all solutions are f(n) = an+b for arbitrary integers a,b.

I2
COMBINATORICS / INVARIANTS

Three lamps are either on or off. One move flips exactly two lamps. Initially only the first lamp is on. Prove all-off is impossible, but all-on is reachable.

Transfer exercise: choose your own tool before checking the hint.

Show first hint

Consider the number of on lamps modulo 2, then give an explicit move.

Show model proof

Flipping two lamps changes the number that are on by −2, 0, or +2. Thus the parity stays odd from the initial state (on, off, off). The all-off state has even parity and is impossible. Flipping lamps 2 and 3 gives (on, on, on), so all-on is reachable. An invariant alone would not prove that reachability.

C1
ALGEBRA / SHARP BOUNDS

Positive x, y, z satisfy x + y + z = 3. Prove xy + yz + zx ≤ 3. State equality.

Transfer exercise: choose your own tool before checking the hint.

Show first hint

Compare x² + y² + z² with xy + yz + zx using three squared differences.

Show model proof

Let P = xy + yz + zx and Q = x² + y² + z². Since (x−y)² + (y−z)² + (z−x)² ≥ 0, we have Q ≥ P. Also Q + 2P = (x+y+z)² = 9. Hence 9 ≥ 3P, so P ≤ 3. Equality requires x=y=z, then each equals 1. The triple (1,1,1) attains the bound.

C2
COMBINATORICS / COUNTEREXAMPLE

Does a positive integer N exist such that EVERY set of N distinct integers contains two whose sum is divisible by 3? Justify your answer.

Transfer exercise: choose your own tool before checking the hint.

Show first hint

Try arbitrarily large sets consisting of numbers in a single residue class.

Show model proof

No such N exists. For every N, choose S = {1,4,7,...,3N−2}. Its N distinct elements are all 1 modulo 3, so any pair sums to 2 modulo 3, not 0. This constructs a counterexample for every N.

Session 2, 20 to 40 minutes: Read the AM-GM lesson, do F1 and, if useful, C1. Ask whether a bound points in the needed direction.

Session 3, 20 to 40 minutes: Practise I2 and C2. Notice what an invariant proves and how a counterexample disproves a universal claim.

Sessions may be split, skipped, repeated or changed. Short written arguments matter more than completing the schedule.

STUDY NOTE

A gentle seven-session plan

A flexible way to learn without making every meeting an examination.

This is a suggested order, not a requirement to finish within seven consecutive days. A session can take about 20 to 40 minutes. Stop earlier if tired.

Session 1: Proof finish line; exercises 11 and 12.

Session 2: Algebra; exercises 1, 2 and 3.

Session 3: Geometry; exercises 4 and 5.

Session 4: Number theory; exercises 6, 7 and 8.

Session 5: Combinatorics; exercises 9 and 10.

Session 6: Choose two earlier proofs. Rewrite them more clearly without opening the model.

Session 7: Review what felt natural, what required hints and which techniques you want to revisit.

A proof-writing template

Claim: I need to show that...

Given: The conditions allow me to assume...

Key idea: The useful observation is...

Justification: This implies the next step because...

Conclusion: Therefore the required statement follows...

Final check: I have included all cases, checked equality or sufficiency where needed and verified the arithmetic.

A useful stopping rule

If an approach stops producing useful facts, write what it established and what remains. You can take a break, request one hint, or switch representation for a stated reason. A timer is optional and says nothing about your ability. A thoughtful partial argument is still valuable learning.
