INDEPENDENT LEARNING / FOUR SMALL PROOFS

The proof is in the missing step.

Try one idea at a time. These exercises are separate from Benchmarks 1-3. Nothing here is scored, and you can open the hints whenever you want.

01 / A LOGIC CHECK

Which conclusion follows?

A student writes: “If x² = y², then x = y.” Is the statement always valid? If not, repair it.

First hint

Try a negative value for one variable.

Worked explanation

It is not always valid. For instance x=3 and y=-3 satisfy x²=y² but x≠y. The correct conclusion is x=y or x=-y, because (x-y)(x+y)=0.

02 / A PROOF FROM PARITY

Three consecutive integers

Prove that among any three consecutive integers, at least one is even and at least one is divisible by 3.

First hint

Let the integers be n, n+1 and n+2; consider remainders.

Worked explanation

Among two consecutive integers exactly one is even, so the first two already contain an even number. Modulo 3 the three consecutive integers have all three residues 0,1,2 in some order, so one is divisible by 3. This holds for positive, negative and zero integers.

03 / COMPLETE THE ARGUMENT

Enough examples?

You test the first ten positive integers and observe a claimed rule always works. Explain why these tests alone do not prove the rule for every integer.

First hint

How many integers have not been tested?

Worked explanation

There are infinitely many positive integers. A rule could hold for the first ten and fail at eleven (for example “n<11”). Examples may help us discover a conjecture, but a universal assertion requires a proof that covers every permitted case.

04 / A CONSTRUCTION CHECK

A point on a circle

A proof introduces a new point F on a circle. Is that automatically invalid because the original question mentioned only points A, B and C? What must be explained?

First hint

A circle has infinitely many points; the important issue is the extra property F is supposed to satisfy.

Worked explanation

Introducing an auxiliary point is allowed. Define it precisely and establish that it exists. If F must also lie on a line or make a particular angle, justify that condition rather than assuming it follows from the diagram.
