01 / FOUNDATIONS

Divisibility is an equation

Write a|b as b=ak for some integer k. This is more useful than saying that a “goes into” b: substitution allows you to prove facts.

If d divides A and B, then d divides uA+vB for every pair of integers u,v. This simple linear-combination rule powers many gcd problems.

d|A and d|B ⇒ d|(uA+vB).

Example: gcd(n,n+1)=1. Any common divisor d divides their difference (n+1)−n=1, so d=1.

Euclid and Bézout

The Euclidean algorithm repeatedly uses gcd(a,b)=gcd(b,a−qb). The final nonzero remainder is the gcd; reversing the steps expresses that gcd as xa+yb for integers x,y. Example: gcd(26,15)=1, and 1=26−(15−(26−15))? Better calculate: 26−15=11, 15−11=4, 11−2·4=3, 4−3=1, hence back-substitution yields an integer combination 1=7·15−4·26. Check 105−104=1.

02 / MODULAR THINKING

Congruences and legal cancellation

a≡b (mod m) means m|(a−b). Addition, subtraction and multiplication preserve congruence. Division is more delicate: from ca≡cb (mod m), you can cancel c only when gcd(c,m)=1, or adjust the modulus appropriately.

2x ≡ 2 (mod 6) ⇒ x ≡ 1 (mod 3), not necessarily (mod 6).

Squares modulo 4 are 0 or 1. A common obstruction: if x²+y²≡3 (mod 4), no integer solutions exist, because the only sums are 0,1 or 2. The proof must state every possible residue.

Residue table exercise

For every integer n, n² is 0 or 1 modulo 3. Show this by cases n≡0,1,2. Consequently 3 cannot divide n²+1, a useful gcd observation.

03 / STRUCTURE

Bound a gcd, then prove when it is attained

Let d=gcd(A(n),B(n)). Find integer coefficients u(n),v(n) for which uA+vB equals a small constant. Then d must divide that constant.

A=n²+n+1, B=2n+1 ⇒ 4A−B²=3.

So d can only be 1 or 3. Separately test n modulo 3 to see when 3 actually divides both A and B. This is a two-way proof: upper restriction and attainment. A proposed gcd is not established merely because it sometimes divides both numbers.

In the Benchmark 3 work, this was a strength: the bound d|3 was found. Improve reliability by closing the residue classes in a clean three-row table.

04 / FACTOR EXPONENTS

Exact powers of primes and induction

For prime p, vₚ(N) denotes the exponent of p dividing a nonzero integer N. Product valuations add: vₚ(ab)=vₚ(a)+vₚ(b). This makes “exactly divisible by 2ⁿ” precise.

Example: for Pₙ=(n+1)···(2n), cancel neighbouring products to find Pₙ₊₁=2(2n+1)Pₙ. Therefore Qₙ=Pₙ/2ⁿ obeys Qₙ₊₁=(2n+1)Qₙ, with Q₁=1. Thus Qₙ is always odd.

Pₙ = 2ⁿ(1·3·5·…·(2n−1)).

The last identity also gives a direct proof. The two proofs use different skills: an induction recurrence and a factor pairing.

05 / INTEGER EQUATIONS

Factorisation and all integer solutions

An equation such as x²−y²=k becomes (x−y)(x+y)=k. But not every factor pair necessarily produces integer x,y: x=(u+v)/2, y=(v−u)/2 require u and v to have the same parity.

For x²−y²=15, the positive ordered factor pairs include (1,15), (3,5), (5,3), (15,1), and their simultaneous negatives; all have odd factors, so all produce integer pairs. You must check the factors and signs rather than announce a count.

06 / DEEP REASONING

Infinite descent and minimal counterexample

To show no positive integer solution exists, suppose one does and choose the smallest suitable solution under a well-ordered measure (often a positive integer size). Produce a strictly smaller positive solution satisfying the same conditions. This contradicts minimality.

Do not casually assert “repeat forever”: you must identify a strictly decreasing positive integer and prove the new object still meets every hypothesis. This method complements induction; the proof runs downward instead of upward.

07 / PRACTICE

Four exercises and model proofs

  1. Prove gcd(n,n+1)=1 for every integer n.
  2. Find gcd(n²+1,n+1) for every integer n.
  3. Prove 30 divides n⁵−n for every integer n.
  4. Determine all integer solutions to x²−y²=8.
Complete worked answers
  1. Any common divisor divides (n+1)−n=1, so the positive gcd is 1.
  2. Reduce n²+1 modulo n+1: n≡−1 gives n²+1≡2. Hence the gcd divides 2. If n is odd, both numbers even, so gcd=2; if n even, n+1 odd, so gcd=1.
  3. Factor n⁵−n=n(n−1)(n+1)(n²+1). Among n−1,n,n+1 one is divisible by 3 and one by 2. Modulo 5, if n≡0,±1 then the first three factors supply 5; if n≡±2 then n²+1≡0. Thus 2,3,5 all divide; they are pairwise coprime, so 30 divides.
  4. (x−y)(x+y)=8. Both factors must share parity; the allowed signed ordered pairs are (2,4),(4,2),(−2,−4),(−4,−2). They produce (x,y)=(3,1),(3,−1),(−3,1),(−3,−1). Check each by substitution.
EXTENDED WORKSHOP 01 / READ, PROVE, TRANSFER

The Euclidean algorithm and Bézout

When to reach for this. Bézout lets you convert a gcd claim into a linear combination and is central to legitimate modular cancellation.

Complete worked argument

The gcd d of integers a,b is the smallest positive integer expressible as xa+yb for integers x,y. The Euclidean algorithm repeatedly replaces (a,b) by (b,a mod b) without changing the set of common divisors; reversing the divisions expresses d as xa+yb. Example 35=2·15+5, so 5=35−2·15, hence gcd(35,15)=5.

The tempting wrong moveClaiming gcd(a,b)=1 does not itself give a concrete cancellation unless you have established coprimality or a Bézout representation.

Transfer question

Find integers u,v with 7u+10v=1 and use them to prove gcd(7,10)=1.

Show a complete answer and proof

Choose u=3,v=−2: 7·3+10(−2)=1. Every common divisor of 7 and 10 divides 1, hence gcd=1. Conversely 1 is trivially a common divisor.

EXTENDED WORKSHOP 02 / READ, PROVE, TRANSFER

Congruence cancellation and prime moduli

When to reach for this. Modulo n, cancelling a factor c is legal only if c has a multiplicative inverse modulo n.

Complete worked argument

If ac≡bc (mod m), then m divides c(a−b). If gcd(c,m)=1, Euclid’s lemma gives m|(a−b), hence a≡b (mod m). If gcd(c,m)>1, cancellation may fail: 2·1≡2·3 (mod 4) but 1≢3 (mod 4).

The tempting wrong moveDividing a congruence by 2 as if it were ordinary equality is invalid modulo an even modulus without further work.

Transfer question

Solve 6x≡6 (mod 15) for residues 0≤x<15.

Show a complete answer and proof

6(x−1)≡0 (mod15) means 15|6(x−1). Divide by gcd(6,15)=3 to obtain 5|2(x−1), and gcd(2,5)=1 gives x≡1 (mod5). Solutions modulo15 are x=1,6,11.

EXTENDED WORKSHOP 03 / READ, PROVE, TRANSFER

Modular obstructions before long equations

When to reach for this. A small modulus can rule out every integer solution without searching any candidates.

Complete worked argument

Squares modulo 4 are 0 or 1. Thus any expression x²+y² cannot be 3 modulo4. This proves x²+y²=4k+3 has no integer solutions for any integer k. For equations involving twice a square, residues modulo8 are often stronger: x² is 0,1 or4 (mod8).

The tempting wrong moveTesting integers from −100 to100 is useful evidence but not a proof about all integers; a congruence argument is finite and exhaustive.

Transfer question

Prove there are no integer solutions of x²+1=3y².

Show a complete answer and proof

Reducing modulo3, x² can be 0 or1, so x²+1 can be1 or2 modulo3, never0. The right side 3y² is0 modulo3. Contradiction.

EXTENDED WORKSHOP 04 / READ, PROVE, TRANSFER

Valuations reveal exact powers

When to reach for this. When a problem asks for the highest power of a prime dividing a product, count prime factors rather than binary digits of the final integer.

Complete worked argument

For a prime p and positive integer n, Legendre’s formula says vₚ(n!)=Σ_{k≥1}⌊n/pᵏ⌋. For p=2,n=10, the sum is ⌊10/2⌋+⌊10/4⌋+⌊10/8⌋=5+2+1=8, hence 10! is divisible by 2⁸ but not by 2⁹. The justification is that every multiple of p contributes one factor, every multiple of p² contributes another and so on.

The tempting wrong moveThe number of 1-bits in the binary expansion does not equal v₂(n) in general: 12=1100₂ has two 1-bits, but v₂(12)=2 by coincidence; 14=1110₂ has three 1-bits yet v₂(14)=1.

Transfer question

Compute v₂(20!) exactly and justify.

Show a complete answer and proof

⌊20/2⌋+⌊20/4⌋+⌊20/8⌋+⌊20/16⌋=10+5+2+1=18, so v₂(20!)=18. Every factor of two is counted at its multiplicity.

EXTENDED WORKSHOP 05 / READ, PROVE, TRANSFER

Infinitely many primes: a minimal complete proof

When to reach for this. A complete contradiction requires constructing a prime not in a proposed finite list, not merely a large composite number.

Complete worked argument

Suppose all primes are p₁,…,pₖ. Form N=p₁p₂⋯pₖ+1. For each listed prime pᵢ, N≡1 (mod pᵢ), so none divides N. Yet N>1 has some prime divisor q. That divisor cannot equal any pᵢ, contradicting completeness. Thus infinitely many primes exist.

The tempting wrong moveThe number N is not necessarily prime (e.g. 2·3·5·7·11·13+1=30031=59·509). The proof needs only a new prime divisor.

Transfer question

Show infinitely many primes are congruent to 3 modulo4.

Show a complete answer and proof

Suppose p₁,…,pₖ are all primes 3 modulo4. Let N=4p₁⋯pₖ−1, which is 3 modulo4 and not divisible by any pᵢ (N≡−1 mod pᵢ). In the prime factorisation of N, an odd number of factors must be 3 modulo4, so there is a new prime of that type. Contradiction.

EXTENDED WORKSHOP 06 / READ, PROVE, TRANSFER

Bounding a gcd with a resultant-like combination

When to reach for this. When d divides two polynomial expressions in n, take a linear combination to create a small constant.

Complete worked argument

For d=gcd(n²+n+1,2n+1), d divides 4(n²+n+1)−(2n+1)²=3. Thus d is either 1 or3. If n≡1 (mod3), then 2n+1≡0 and n²+n+1≡0, so d=3. Otherwise 2n+1 or n²+n+1 is not divisible by3, hence d=1. Both divisibility and exclusion are proven.

The tempting wrong moveShowing d divides 3 does not show that d equals3 for some n; always check the exceptional class achieves the larger value.

Transfer question

Determine gcd(n²+n+1,n+2) for every integer n.

Show a complete answer and proof

Modulo n+2, set n≡−2. Then n²+n+1≡4−2+1=3. Therefore gcd divides3. If n≡1 (mod3), n+2 and n²+n+1 are divisible by3 so gcd=3; otherwise gcd=1.

EXTENDED WORKSHOP 07 / READ, PROVE, TRANSFER

The Chinese remainder theorem as a construction

When to reach for this. Congruence constraints can be combined to describe a unique residue class when moduli are coprime.

Complete worked argument

Solve x≡1 (mod3), x≡2 (mod5): put x=1+3k. Then 1+3k≡2 (mod5), so 3k≡1 and k≡2 (mod5). Hence x≡7 (mod15). Coprimality ensures this solution class modulo15 is unique. A solution to simultaneous congruences must satisfy every modulus, so always verify.

The tempting wrong moveIf moduli share factors, two congruences may be incompatible. For instance x≡0 (mod2) and x≡1 (mod4) have no common solution.

Transfer question

Solve x≡2 (mod3) and x≡3 (mod7).

Show a complete answer and proof

Write x=2+3k. Mod7, 3k≡1; inverse of3 is5, so k≡5 (mod7). Hence x≡17 (mod21). Check 17≡2 mod3 and17≡3 mod7.
