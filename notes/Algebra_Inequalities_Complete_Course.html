01 / STRUCTURE

Factor before you expand

Before expanding a complicated equation, ask whether the expression has a difference of squares, symmetric sums or a useful substitution.

For integer x,y, x²−y²=3(x+y)+10 becomes (x+y)(x−y−3)=10. Factor-pair classification gives eight solutions; just as importantly, the reverse substitution establishes integrality and completeness.

02 / INEQUALITIES

Cauchy–Schwarz with hypotheses

For real vectors u,v, (Σuᵢvᵢ)²≤(Σuᵢ²)(Σvᵢ²). Equality holds when one vector is a scalar multiple of the other. To prove (a²+b²+c²)²≤a³+b³+c³ under a+b+c=1, choose u=(a3/2,...) and v=(a1/2,...). Nonnegativity is essential.

(Σa²)² ≤ (Σa³)(Σa) = Σa³.

Equality forces positive coordinates to be equal, but does not force every coordinate positive. Thus the zero-coordinate cases are indispensable.

03 / INEQUALITIES

AM–GM and the equality trap

For nonnegative x,y, (x+y)/2≥√(xy), with equality iff x=y. The proof is (√x−√y)²≥0. With three nonnegative numbers, (x+y+z)/3≥(xyz)1/3; equality when x=y=z.

Do not invoke AM–GM on terms with unproven signs. If x+y is fixed, xy≤(x+y)²/4 follows by completing a square; identify equality at x=y.

04 / STRATEGY

Symmetry is a clue, not a proof

A symmetric problem may suggest the candidate a=b=c, but its extrema can lie on boundaries, as the Benchmark 3 equality classification shows. Write “test equal values and boundary values” before assuming they are the only cases.

For a+b+c=1 with nonnegative variables, the domain is a closed triangle including its edges and vertices. A continuous symmetric expression can attain an extremum on an edge, so do not neglect zero entries.

05 / SEQUENCES

Induction, recurrence and invariants

A sequence recurrence should be written with a base value and an induction step that covers every permitted index. If Qₙ₊₁=(2n+1)Qₙ and Q₁=1, oddness propagates.

When you find a pattern, explicitly prove it is invariant under the transition. A few computed terms can suggest a claim but never prove the full recurrence.

06 / PRACTICE

Five problems with checks

  1. Prove xy≤(x+y)²/4 for x,y≥0 and classify equality.
  2. Under a+b+c=1 and a,b,c≥0, find all equality cases of (Σa²)²≤Σa³.
  3. Find all integer pairs satisfying x²−y²=15.
  4. Given a₁=2, aₙ₊₁=3aₙ+1, find a closed formula and prove it.
  5. For nonnegative a,b,c summing to 1, show a²+b²+c²≥1/3 and identify equality.
Solutions
  1. (x−y)²≥0 ⇒ (x+y)²≥4xy, equality iff x=y.
  2. Cauchy and all permutations of (1,0,0),(½,½,0),(⅓,⅓,⅓); verify all.
  3. Factor (x−y)(x+y)=15. Enumerate odd signed factors and recover x,y with x=(u+v)/2,y=(v−u)/2.
  4. aₙ=(5·3ⁿ⁻¹−1)/2. Check n=1 and substitute into recurrence.
  5. Cauchy: (a+b+c)²≤3(a²+b²+c²), so Σa²≥1/3; equality iff a=b=c=1/3.
EXTENDED WORKSHOP 01 / READ, PROVE, TRANSFER

Cauchy–Schwarz: equality is structural

When to reach for this. When a squared sum is bounded by a product of two sums, matching vectors often reveal the exact equality cases.

Complete worked argument

For nonnegative a,b,c with sum1, apply Cauchy to (a^{3/2},b^{3/2},c^{3/2}) and (a^{1/2},b^{1/2},c^{1/2}): (a²+b²+c²)²≤(a³+b³+c³)(a+b+c)=a³+b³+c³. Equality iff the two vectors are proportional. On each positive coordinate this forces a constant ratio a^{3/2}/a^{1/2}=a. Hence all positive coordinates are equal. If k of them are positive, each is 1/k; k=1,2,3. Check permutations of (1,0,0),(1/2,1/2,0),(1/3,1/3,1/3).

The tempting wrong moveDividing by a coordinate before checking if it is zero loses boundary equality cases and may incorrectly claim only a=b=c.

Transfer question

Under the same assumptions, prove a²+b²+c²≥1/3 and classify equality.

Show a complete answer and proof

Cauchy applied to (a,b,c) and (1,1,1) gives (a+b+c)²≤3(a²+b²+c²), so Σa²≥1/3. Equality iff (a,b,c) is proportional to (1,1,1), and sum1 gives all 1/3.

EXTENDED WORKSHOP 02 / READ, PROVE, TRANSFER

AM–GM with signs and zero cases

When to reach for this. AM–GM requires nonnegative inputs and sharpness requires an equality construction.

Complete worked argument

For real x,y≥0, (√x−√y)²≥0 gives x+y≥2√(xy), with equality iff x=y. For x+y=10, the product xy≤25, with equality at x=y=5. Do not use AM–GM for arbitrary negative reals without an independent argument allowing such inputs.

The tempting wrong moveApplying a nonnegative inequality to terms whose signs are unknown can reverse or invalidate the conclusion.

Transfer question

For a,b,c>0 with abc=1, prove a+b+c≥3 and identify equality.

Show a complete answer and proof

AM–GM: (a+b+c)/3≥(abc)^{1/3}=1. Equality iff a=b=c, and product1 gives a=b=c=1.

EXTENDED WORKSHOP 03 / READ, PROVE, TRANSFER

Vieta controls integer root possibilities

When to reach for this. Sum and product of roots can turn a polynomial equation into arithmetic constraints, especially for integral solutions.

Complete worked argument

For x²−Sx+P=0 with roots r,s, expanding (x−r)(x−s)=x²−(r+s)x+rs shows r+s=S and rs=P. If roots are integers then their product divides P, severely limiting candidates. For x²−5x+6=0, r+s=5 and rs=6, so r=2,s=3. Verify substitution.

The tempting wrong moveVieta describes roots counting multiplicity; concluding “two distinct real roots” requires a positive discriminant, and an integer-root search should include negative divisors.

Transfer question

Find all integer roots of x²+x−12=0 using factor pairs.

Show a complete answer and proof

Factor x²+x−12=(x+4)(x−3), so x=−4 or x=3. Both satisfy the equation and the factorisation proves no others.

EXTENDED WORKSHOP 04 / READ, PROVE, TRANSFER

Finite differences can reveal a polynomial

When to reach for this. A sequence with constant first differences is affine; constant second differences suggest a quadratic.

Complete worked argument

If aₙ=n², first differences a_{n+1}−aₙ=2n+1 vary linearly, while the second difference (a_{n+2}−a_{n+1})−(a_{n+1}−aₙ)=2 is constant. Conversely, if a sequence on all integers has second differences constantly2, subtract n² to produce a sequence with zero second differences, hence an affine sequence bn+c. Thus aₙ=n²+bn+c, determined by two starting values.

The tempting wrong moveAn observed finite-difference pattern over three data points suggests a polynomial but does not prove all subsequent terms follow it without a recurrence assumption.

Transfer question

Given a₀=3,a₁=6 and a_{n+2}−2a_{n+1}+aₙ=2 for all n≥0, find aₙ.

Show a complete answer and proof

Set aₙ=n²+bn+c. Then c=a₀=3, and a₁=1+b+3=6 gives b=2. Hence aₙ=n²+2n+3; this satisfies the recurrence and initial conditions. Uniqueness follows since each next term is determined by the previous two.

EXTENDED WORKSHOP 05 / READ, PROVE, TRANSFER

Symmetry versus an equality claim

When to reach for this. A symmetric expression has symmetric solutions, but symmetry alone does not force every solution to have equal variables.

Complete worked argument

Consider x+y=4 and xy=3. The equations are symmetric in x,y, so swapping any solution produces another. Their solutions are (1,3) and (3,1), not x=y. In inequalities, symmetry can motivate trying a=b=c as a candidate, but zero boundaries and unequal critical points must be checked. The correct test is algebraic: complete squares, apply sharp inequalities, analyse equality hypotheses.

The tempting wrong move“By symmetry assume a≥b≥c” is permitted for fully symmetric conditions and conclusion, but not for an expression whose terms treat variables differently.

Transfer question

Given a,b≥0, a+b=1, find the maximum of ab and determine equality.

Show a complete answer and proof

(a−b)²≥0 gives (a+b)²≥4ab, so ab≤1/4. Equality iff a=b=1/2. This proves both the universal bound and its attainment.

EXTENDED WORKSHOP 06 / READ, PROVE, TRANSFER

Induction is a chain, not a pattern

When to reach for this. The first few examples may be convincing, but induction must establish a universally valid transition.

Complete worked argument

To prove 1+3+⋯+(2n−1)=n² for n≥1, verify n=1. Assume the sum is n² for a fixed n. Adding the next term 2n+1 yields n²+2n+1=(n+1)². Therefore by induction all positive n satisfy the identity. Notice that “it seems to work for many n” is replaced by a precise implication P(n)⇒P(n+1).

The tempting wrong moveThe induction hypothesis must apply to the quantity being transformed; do not silently assume a stronger statement than the one established.

Transfer question

Prove Σ_{k=1}^{n}k=n(n+1)/2 for all n≥1.

Show a complete answer and proof

At n=1, both sides are 1. If true for n, adding n+1 gives n(n+1)/2+(n+1)=(n+1)(n+2)/2. This is exactly the formula at n+1. Hence true for all positive n.
