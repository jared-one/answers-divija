01 / COUNTING DISCIPLINE

Define the object before counting

Ask: what are the objects? Are they ordered? Are repetitions allowed? What makes two outcomes different? A formula only counts correctly when each eligible outcome appears the intended number of times.

For a pattern AAAABC, choose the repeated value (11 ways), then choose the other two as an unordered pair (C(10,2) ways). The total 495 is independent of how you label B and C.

11×C(10,2)=495; not 11×10×9/3!.

Three score blocks have 3! abstract permutations, but the decreasing-score condition permits exactly one valid block order. Do not confuse label-selection overcounts with rearrangements of already chosen blocks.

02 / DIRECT COUNTING

Stars and bars versus case splitting

If six marks are chosen from 11 scores 0..10 with repeats allowed and then forced into decreasing order, the problem is equivalent to choosing nonnegative frequencies x₀,...,x₁₀ summing to six. Six stars and ten bars produce C(16,6)=8008 outcomes.

x₀+···+x₁₀=6 ⇒ C(16,10)=8008.

Case splitting by the number of distinct marks also works: when exactly k distinct marks occur, choose them in C(11,k) ways and choose a composition of six into k positive multiplicities in C(5,k−1) ways. The sum over k=1,…,6 is 8008. This is slower but teaches structural completeness.

03 / PIGEONHOLE

Choose the right buckets

Ten whole numbers in 1..18 force a pair summing to 19 because the nine complementary pairs (1,18),(2,17),…,(9,10) are nine buckets. Ten chosen numbers occupy two slots of one bucket.

For the Benchmark 3 consecutive-sum claim, use prefix sums rather than individual terms. Either a prefix is 0 modulo n, or n nonzero prefixes occupy only n−1 residue classes and two coincide.

04 / ORDERING

Increasing and decreasing subsequences

For ten distinct numbers, suppose there is no increasing or decreasing subsequence of length four. At each position i record (pᵢ,qᵢ), the lengths of longest increasing and decreasing subsequences ending at i. Both lengths are at most three, leaving nine state pairs.

Ten positions force a repeated pair. For i<j, distinctness gives xᵢ<xⱼ or xᵢ>xⱼ. The first extends an increasing subsequence, the second a decreasing subsequence, contradicting equality of state pairs.

Sharpness: 3,2,1,6,5,4,9,8,7 has no such length-four subsequence. This is the finite instance of the Erdős–Szekeres theorem.

05 / TWO COUNTS

Double counting and graph arguments

In any finite undirected graph, the sum of vertex degrees equals twice the number of edges, because every edge contributes two endpoints. This handshaking lemma immediately implies that the number of odd-degree vertices is even.

A stronger olympiad technique is to count the same collection of pairs or incidences in two different ways. State exactly what is being counted by both methods; mere equality of two formulas is not enough.

06 / GLOBAL CONSTRAINTS

Invariants, monovariants and extremal arguments

An invariant is unchanged by every legal move; a monovariant moves in one direction. For a chessboard with two same-colour opposite corners removed, domino tiling is impossible: a domino always covers one black and one white square, but the altered board has unequal colour counts.

For minimum/maximum problems, prove both the bound and a construction achieving it. An example showing nine terms are not enough does not prove ten terms always work.

07 / PRACTICE

Five proof-based practice questions

  1. How many nonincreasing sequences of length four use only 0,1,2,3?
  2. Show that among any five integers, two have the same remainder modulo 4.
  3. Prove that every undirected graph has an even number of odd-degree vertices.
  4. Prove any sequence of five distinct real numbers has an increasing or decreasing subsequence of length three.
  5. For four different score labels with multiplicity pattern 2+2+1+1, how many distinct descending sequences can be formed using scores 0,…,10?
Answers and proofs
  1. Stars/bars: C(4+4−1,4)=C(7,4)=35.
  2. Four residue buckets for five integers: pigeonhole forces two in one bucket.
  3. Degree sum equals 2|E|, an even integer; the number of odd terms in this sum must be even.
  4. At each index track longest increasing and decreasing subsequences. If each ≤2 there are four pairs, so five indices force two matching; ordering makes one length grow, contradiction.
  5. Choose the four distinct scores in C(11,4) ways, then choose which two get multiplicity two: C(4,2) ways. The remaining two are singletons, so the answer is 330×6=1980. Descending order is unique.
EXTENDED WORKSHOP 01 / READ, PROVE, TRANSFER

When not to divide by a factorial

When to reach for this. There are two independent combinatorial actions: assigning roles to chosen values, and ordering those values in an output. Only actual overcounting licenses division.

Complete worked argument

For six descending marks with pattern AAAABC (four equal and two distinct singletons), choose A in 11 ways and choose the two other score values as an unordered pair in C(10,2) ways. This is 495. If instead choosing ordered labels A,B,C, the raw 11·10·9 counts each final sequence exactly twice because exchanging B and C changes no output. Dividing by 3! would incorrectly identify different choices of which score is repeated four times.

The tempting wrong move“There are three blocks, therefore divide by 3!” is wrong because the decreasing rule already fixes the block order. Factorial division concerns duplicate counted outcomes, not simply available blocks.

Transfer question

For a pattern AAAABB (four copies of A and two of B) from score values 0,…,10, count descending sequences.

Show a complete answer and proof

Choose A in 11 ways and B in 10 different ways. Different assignments produce different repetition counts and hence different outputs, so no division: 110 sequences.

EXTENDED WORKSHOP 02 / READ, PROVE, TRANSFER

Inclusion-exclusion and disjoint case audits

When to reach for this. Use inclusion-exclusion when two counted events overlap; correct overcounting systematically instead of subtracting guesses.

Complete worked argument

For integers 1..100, count divisible by 2 or3. Multiples of2: 50. Multiples of3:33. Multiples of6 counted twice:16. Total 50+33−16=67. For three sets A,B,C, inclusion-exclusion adds singles, subtracts pairs, then adds the triple overlap once.

The tempting wrong moveMake sure intersections use lcm for divisibility, e.g. divisible by4 and6 means divisible by12, not24.

Transfer question

How many numbers in {1,…,120} are divisible by 4 or 6?

Show a complete answer and proof

There are 30 multiples of4, 20 multiples of6 and 10 multiples of lcm(4,6)=12. Thus 30+20−10=40.

EXTENDED WORKSHOP 03 / READ, PROVE, TRANSFER

Prefix sums detect contiguous structures

When to reach for this. For statements about consecutive terms, partial sums turn a long block into the difference of two numbers.

Complete worked argument

Given n integers a₁,…,aₙ, define prefix sums S₀=0 and Sₖ=a₁+⋯+aₖ. Consider residues of S₀,…,Sₙ modulo n, a total of n+1 residues in n classes. Two are equal; their difference is a contiguous sum aᵢ+⋯+aⱼ divisible by n. This proof is one line after the right representation is chosen.

The tempting wrong moveChoosing n individual terms with matching residues does not necessarily give a consecutive block; prefix sums are the essential change of representation.

Transfer question

Prove that among any seven integers in order, some nonempty consecutive block has sum divisible by7.

Show a complete answer and proof

Take S₀=0,S₁,…,S₇. Eight prefix sums occupy seven residues mod7, so Sⱼ≡Sᵢ for some 0≤i<j≤7. Then a_{i+1}+⋯+aⱼ=Sⱼ−Sᵢ is divisible by7.

EXTENDED WORKSHOP 04 / READ, PROVE, TRANSFER

Erdős–Szekeres needs the two-coordinate state

When to reach for this. The right invariant of a sequence position is not one longest subsequence length, but two.

Complete worked argument

For N=(r−1)(s−1)+1 distinct numbers, if there is no increasing subsequence of length r and no decreasing one of length s, each position i has an associated pair (pᵢ,qᵢ) with 1≤pᵢ≤r−1 and 1≤qᵢ≤s−1. There are at most (r−1)(s−1) pairs, but N positions. Two indices i<j share a pair. If xᵢ<xⱼ, append xⱼ to a longest increasing subsequence ending at i; otherwise append it to a longest decreasing one. Either case makes one component larger at j, contradiction.

The tempting wrong moveFinding a nine-term counterexample does not prove every ten-term sequence works. The state-pigeonhole proof supplies the missing universal direction.

Transfer question

Prove any sequence of seven distinct numbers contains an increasing subsequence of length four or a decreasing subsequence of length three.

Show a complete answer and proof

Take r=4,s=3. (r−1)(s−1)+1=3·2+1=7. Apply the pair-state proof: otherwise each position has 1≤p≤3 and1≤q≤2, only six possible states; seven positions force a repeat and contradiction.

EXTENDED WORKSHOP 05 / READ, PROVE, TRANSFER

A complete sharp maximum argument

When to reach for this. The words maximum or minimum require a universal bound and an attainable construction.

Complete worked argument

Suppose ten people are split into two groups of five and friendship only occurs between groups. There are 5×5=25 friendships and no triangle. For any triangle-free graph on ten vertices, Mantel’s theorem gives at most⌊10²/4⌋=25 edges. The deeper proof can be obtained by deleting vertices of maximum degree or by edge-sum inequalities, but simply drawing the bipartition establishes only the lower bound 25.

The tempting wrong moveThe correct example does not prove optimality: without a universal upper bound there might be another arrangement with more edges.

Transfer question

On six vertices, construct a triangle-free graph with nine edges. Explain why it is triangle-free.

Show a complete answer and proof

Take the complete bipartite graph K₃,₃. There are three vertices in each group, and every one of the 3×3 cross-group pairs is an edge, giving nine. Any triangle would require two vertices in the same group joined by an edge, but none exists.

EXTENDED WORKSHOP 06 / READ, PROVE, TRANSFER

Ramsey-style reasoning: six people always force a triangle

When to reach for this. A globally forced configuration often emerges from choosing one point and applying pigeonhole to its local neighbours.

Complete worked argument

Colour each edge among six vertices red or blue. Choose vertex v; among its five incident edges, at least three share a colour. Say v is connected to a,b,c by red edges. If any of ab,ac,bc is red, it forms a red triangle with v. Otherwise all three edges among a,b,c are blue, forming a blue triangle. Thus every two-colouring of K₆ has a monochromatic triangle.

The tempting wrong moveThe theorem is not saying every set of six people has three mutual friends: the conclusion is either three mutual friends or three mutual strangers.

Transfer question

Explain why five people need not force a triangle of friends or a triangle of strangers.

Show a complete answer and proof

Place five vertices on a cycle, colour the five cycle edges red and the five diagonals blue. Each colour forms a 5-cycle and neither colour has a triangle. Hence six is sharp.

EXTENDED WORKSHOP 07 / READ, PROVE, TRANSFER

Invariants, weighted sums and sufficiency

When to reach for this. An invariant proves impossibility, but to classify achievable states you may also need an explicit sequence of legal moves.

Complete worked argument

A weighted sum I=Σwᵢsᵢ modulo m is invariant if every legal move changes I by a multiple of m. In the BMO1 2019 sweets problem, a suitable weighting detects divisibility by28. But this only gives necessity: it forbids some n. To conclude all multiples of28 work, one must supply a legal construction of the final equal distribution. The marked companion does both.

The tempting wrong moveA parity invariant alone can sometimes be too weak: multiple end states may have the same parity. Weighted or multi-coordinate invariants can distinguish them.

Transfer question

You may flip exactly two of three lamps per move, starting with all off. Can you reach exactly one lamp on?

Show a complete answer and proof

The parity of the total number of lit lamps is invariant: flipping two changes the total by −2,0 or2, always even. Initially zero lamps are on (even). Exactly one would be odd, so impossible.
