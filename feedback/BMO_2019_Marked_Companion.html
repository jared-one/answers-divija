Prime configurations

2/10Recorded partial-credit decision

What your work established

Your page includes examples and modular observations, but does not give the required three qualifying examples and then a complete uniqueness argument for the longer prime pattern.

Where marks were lost

Missing a verified third example and an exhaustive proof for the five-prime configuration.

The /10 value follows the earlier examiner-style review; there is no verified official step-by-step allocation for this photographed response.

Full model proof and critical notes

Part one. The three requested primes p below 200 can be taken as 5, 11 and 101. Their associated sets are respectively (5,7,11,13,17), (11,13,17,19,23) and (101,103,107,109,113); each entry is prime. Verify primality of each required entry by trial division up to its square root.

Part two. For the longer pattern, reduce the six offsets 0,2,6,8,12,14 modulo 5. They occupy all five residue classes 0,1,2,3,4. Therefore one of q, q+2, q+6, q+8, q+12, q+14 is a multiple of 5. Because all six are prime, that multiple must equal 5. Thus q≤5; the only prime candidates are 2,3,5. For q=2, q+2=4 is not prime; for q=3, q+6=9 is not prime. For q=5, the six values 5,7,11,13,17,19 are all prime. Hence q=5 is unique.

The key proof structure is: exhibit the three examples, force a small candidate list, eliminate all but one, and verify the survivor.

The integer sequence

Where marks were lost

No material gap. Negative odd starting values are included.

Full model proof and critical notes

Rearrange the recurrence into (2aₙ₊₁−aₙ)²=1, hence aₙ₊₁=(aₙ±1)/2. Since aₙ₊₁ is an integer, aₙ must be odd. In particular, a₁ must be odd.

Conversely, suppose aₙ is odd. Then (aₙ+1)/2 and (aₙ−1)/2 are consecutive integers; exactly one is odd. Choose the odd one for aₙ₊₁. This creates an odd integer successor indefinitely and satisfies the original recurrence. So every odd integer can be a₁, and no even integer can.

Full model proof and critical notes

Here is an alternative complete solution using a homothety and Thales' theorem. Because the two circles touch externally at P, there is a negative homothety centred at P taking the first circle to the second. Let A′ be the image of A. The image of the tangent at A is a tangent at A′ parallel to AB. For the external common tangent, A′ is the antipodal point of B on the second circle (the negative homothety reverses the perpendicular-radius direction).

Since C,P,D are collinear and C,D are the non-P intersections of the same line with the respective circles, C is sent to D by this homothety. Consequently the chord AC is mapped to A′D, hence AC∥A′D.

Now BA′ is a diameter of the second circle. Therefore ∠BDA′=90° by the angle-in-a-semicircle theorem. Since A′D∥AC, we conclude BD⊥AC.

This is a different rigorous solution from the angle-chasing route attempted in your script. The important geometric fact is the homothety between externally tangent circles; the official report can be used to compare an elementary angle-based proof.

The penguin queue

4/10Recorded partial-credit decision

What your work established

Where marks were lost

The /10 value follows the earlier examiner-style review; there is no verified official step-by-step allocation for this photographed response.

Full model proof and critical notes

Think of the queue as a rooted tree. The parent of ticket n>1 is its largest proper divisor, i.e. n divided by its least prime factor. Each ticket is inserted immediately after its parent. By induction over arriving tickets, the final queue is a depth-first preorder traversal in which children of any parent appear in decreasing numerical order (because the latest arriving child goes immediately after the parent).

Part (a). Ticket 2 is the last child of ticket 1 because the children of 1 are primes in descending order. Its entire subtree comprises the powers of two, namely 2,4,8,…,1024, exactly ten tickets. Therefore every other ticket lies in front of ticket 2: 2019−10=2009.

Part (b), behind 33. Tickets 66 and 99 are the children of 33: the least prime factor of 66 is 2, and of 99 is 3. Ticket 99 is larger and so is the first child in the final preorder, immediately behind 33.

Part (b), in front of 33. Ticket 33 is a child of 11; the immediately preceding child-subtree belongs to ticket 55 (the children of 11 are visited in decreasing order 121,77,55,33,22, within the limit). The last branch of the subtree rooted at 55 repeatedly takes the smallest child 55→110→220→440→880→1760. This final ticket is the one immediately in front of 33. Hence the neighbours are 1760 (front) and 99 (behind). The proof explains why later arrivals do not change the final order in the way a partial simulation might suggest.

BMO19-05 / BMO1 2019

Perfect sweets distribution

3/10Recorded partial-credit decision

What your work established

You identify the correct multiples-of-28 answer and some move/invariant thinking.

Where marks were lost

The proof of necessity is incomplete: you have not shown that every other positive number of sweets is impossible.

The /10 value follows the earlier examiner-style review; there is no verified official step-by-step allocation for this photographed response.

Full model proof and critical notes

Label the six seats 0,1,2,3,4,5 around the circle, with all n sweets initially at seat 0. Let sᵢ be the current number of sweets at seat i. Consider the integer-weighted quantity

I = s₀ + 6s₁ − 6s₂ − s₃ + s₄ − s₅.

Each legal move at a seat removes four sweets there and adds one sweet to each of its two neighbours and opposite seat. Check the change in I for each of the six possible move seats. The changes are 0, −28, +28, 0, 0, 0, respectively. Consequently I modulo 28 is invariant under every legal move.

At the beginning I=n. In any equal final distribution all seats contain k sweets, so I=k(1+6−6−1+1−1)=0. Therefore n≡0 (mod 28): necessity.

Sufficiency. Suppose n=28t for an integer t≥1. Move at seat 0 exactly 7t times. Each move is legal while performed: seat 0 starts with 28t and loses four sweets each time. Afterwards seat 0 has 0; seats 1,3,5 have 7t sweets each; seats 2,4 have 0. Now move at each of seats 1,3,5 exactly t times. These are legal because each such seat has at least 7t−4(t−1)≥4 sweets before its last move. None of these three seats is a neighbour or opposite of another of the three, so they do not supply sweets to each other. Each ends with 3t sweets, while seats 0,2,4 each receive one sweet from each of the 3t total moves, giving 3t sweets. Thus all six seats finish with 3t sweets. Hence every positive multiple of 28 is perfect.

We have proved n is perfect if and only if 28 divides n. The weighted invariant is not something you must guess instantly; the reusable insight is to search for a conserved quantity when local moves create global restrictions.

Full model proof and critical notes

Start with the published relation, which has one equality linking f(m,n) to f(m−n,n−m) and another linking it to shifted inputs. Define g(t)=f(t,−t). The first equality becomes 2f(m,n)=g(m−n)+m+n.

Applying the second equality and substituting this expression yields g(t+1)+g(t−1)=2g(t): consecutive differences are constant. Hence g(t)=kt+b for all integers t. Evaluating the first identity at (0,0) shows b=0; also g(2t)=2g(t), consistently.

Therefore f(m,n)=[k(m−n)+m+n]/2. It is an integer for every integer m,n exactly when k is odd, say k=2r+1. Thus f(m,n)=(r+1)m−rn for any integer r. Direct substitution verifies both original equalities, completing necessity and sufficiency. You can work backwards through this solution in the dedicated Functional Equations course.
