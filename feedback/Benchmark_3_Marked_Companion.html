Why this matters

Every integer solution must supply an integer factor pair of 10; your table covers them.

Full model proof and critical notes

Rearrange the given equation as (x+y)(x−y−3)=10. Put u=x+y and v=x−y−3. Then uv=10.

Because 10 has exactly the eight signed, ordered factor pairs (1,10), (2,5), (5,2), (10,1) and their simultaneous negatives, there are no additional possibilities for (u,v).

We recover x=(u+v+3)/2 and y=(u−v−3)/2. In each allowed factor pair one factor is even and the other odd; consequently x and y are integers. Substitution gives the eight ordered pairs:

(−4,−6), (−4,3), (−2,−3), (−2,0),
(5,−3), (5,0), (7,−6), (7,3)

The equivalence of the factorisation proves that all the listed pairs work and that no ninth pair exists.

Why this matters

Length of algebraic work is not evidence of a proved inequality; the key is a correct theorem with applicable hypotheses.

Full model proof and critical notes

For nonnegative a,b,c with a+b+c=1, write a²=a3/2a1/2 and similarly for b,c. Cauchy–Schwarz gives

(a²+b²+c²)² ≤ (a³+b³+c³)(a+b+c) = a³+b³+c³.

The nonnegativity condition is needed for the square roots. Equality in Cauchy–Schwarz holds exactly when the vectors (a3/2,b3/2,c3/2) and (a1/2,b1/2,c1/2) are proportional.

For every positive coordinate t this means t3/2=λt1/2, hence t=λ: all positive coordinates are equal. If exactly k coordinates are positive, each equals 1/k. Since k=1,2,3, the equality cases are precisely all permutations of

(1,0,0), (½,½,0), (⅓,⅓,⅓).

These satisfy the original inequality with equality, completing both directions.

Why this matters

A drawing cannot certify concyclicity; the proof must identify the angle theorem and show its hypotheses.

Full model proof and critical notes

It suffices to reflect H in the side BC, obtaining H′. Since BH is perpendicular to AC and CH is perpendicular to AB,

∠HBC=90°−∠ACB and ∠HCB=90°−∠ABC.

Angle sum in triangle BHC gives ∠BHC=180°−∠BAC. Reflection across BC fixes B,C and preserves the angle at H, so ∠BH′C=180°−∠BAC. Because ABC is acute, H is inside the triangle and its reflection H′ is across BC from A. Thus ∠BAC+∠BH′C=180°.

By the converse cyclic-quadrilateral theorem, A,B,H′,C lie on one circle. This is exactly the circumcircle of ABC. Repeating the identical argument after relabelling the vertices handles reflections across AB and CA.

Full model proof and critical notes

Let P=AC∩BD. Since A,B,C,D are concyclic, corresponding inscribed angles yield △PAB∼△PDC. The ratio PA/PD=PB/PC therefore gives

PA·PC=PB·PD.

If AB∥CD, alternate interior angles also give △PAB∼△PCD, with a different vertex correspondence. Hence PA/PC=PB/PD=r for some r>0.

Thus PA=rPC and PB=rPD. Insert these into the previous identity: r(PC)²=r(PD)², so PC=PD because lengths are positive, and then PA=PB. Both claims follow.

Critical habit: state correspondence for each similarity; the two pairs of triangles are not interchangeable.

Why this matters

Testing examples, interpreting binary digits and informal counting do not prove oddness universally.

Full model proof and critical notes

Let Pₙ=(n+1)(n+2)···(2n) and Qₙ=Pₙ/2ⁿ. At n=1, Q₁=1 is odd.

Cancel common factors in Pₙ₊₁/Pₙ:

Pₙ₊₁ = Pₙ · (2n+1)(2n+2)/(n+1) = 2(2n+1)Pₙ.

Divide by 2ⁿ⁺¹: Qₙ₊₁=(2n+1)Qₙ. If Qₙ is an odd integer, then 2n+1 and Qₙ are odd integers, so their product Qₙ₊₁ is odd. The base case and induction step prove the statement for all n≥1.

An alternative direct proof is Pₙ=2ⁿ(1·3·5···(2n−1)).

Why this matters

There is a correct decisive restriction and a substantially correct classification, so this earns serious partial credit.

Full model proof and critical notes

Let A=n²+n+1, B=2n+1 and d=gcd(A,B)>0. As d divides both A and B, it also divides the combination

4A−B² = 4(n²+n+1)−(2n+1)²=3.

Therefore d is 1 or 3, no matter whether n is positive, negative or zero. If n≡1 (mod 3), both A and B are divisible by 3, so d=3. If n≡0 or 2 (mod 3), then B≢0 (mod 3), so d=1.

d = 3 when n≡1 (mod 3); otherwise d = 1.

The three residues 0,1,2 exhaust all integers, including negative integers.

Full model proof and critical notes

Define Sₖ=a₁+···+aₖ for k=1,2,…,n. If any Sₖ≡0 (mod n), then the block from a₁ through aₖ works; it has at least one term.

Ten numbers, or nine?

This rubric mapping reconstructs the earlier marking decision from the photographed work and the companion rubric; it is not a literal official BMO mark sheet.

Why this matters

The example shows the bound is sharp, but without a universal guarantee it is only half of the result.

Full model proof and critical notes

At every position i, define pᵢ as the length of a longest strictly increasing subsequence ending at i, and qᵢ similarly for a decreasing subsequence. Assume neither type has length 4. Then pᵢ and qᵢ are each among 1,2,3, so there are only nine possible ordered pairs (pᵢ,qᵢ).

There are ten positions. Two indices i<j must share the same pair. If xᵢ<xⱼ, an increasing subsequence ending at i extends to j, forcing pⱼ≥pᵢ+1. If xᵢ>xⱼ, the decreasing analogue gives qⱼ≥qᵢ+1. Both contradict equality of the pairs.

Thus ten forces a length-four increasing or decreasing subsequence. For nine, take

3,2,1, 6,5,4, 9,8,7.

An increasing subsequence uses at most one element from each three-term decreasing block, and a decreasing subsequence stays within a single block. Each has length at most three, proving sharpness.

Repair exerciseBefore looking at the proof, determine how many (increasing length, decreasing length) pairs are possible when neither reaches four.
