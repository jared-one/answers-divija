ALGEBRA 1

Functional equations · Benchmark 2

What you established: You worked out a general formula from a recurrence, and your argument considered both positive and negative integers. That is meaningful work.

What remains: The final check uses selected inputs rather than checking the original equation for every integer pair x, y.

See how to complete the proof
  1. Let h(n) = f(n) − n2. Setting y = 1 in the original equation shows that the consecutive differences of h are constant.
  2. Thus the only possible functions have the form f(n) = n2 + an + b for integers a, b. You obtained the same family using your own constants.
  3. Now check an arbitrary pair x, y:
    4. f(x+y) + f(x−y) = 2x2 + 2y2 + 2ax + 2b = 2f(x) + 2y2.

    Now you have both halves: every solution must be on your list, and every function on the list really works.

Think about: Can you write the full substitution without looking at this explanation?

Inequalities · Benchmark 2

What you established: You correctly cleared the positive denominators and reached the relevant polynomial expression. That was a valid step.

What remains: The later factoring did not establish non-negativity or the equality case. You also reported a factorisation hint, so this page is best treated as assisted practice.

See how to complete the proof
  1. For any positive x and y, put both sides of the following difference over the same positive denominator:
  2. x2/(x+y) − (3x−y)/4.
  3. The numerator becomes a perfect square. This provides a non-negative term for each cyclic pair (a,b), (b,c), (c,a).

GEOMETRY 2

Cyclic quadrilaterals · Benchmark 2

What you established: You explored the cyclic diagram and several angle relationships. Exploring multiple paths is a normal part of geometry problem solving.

What remains: The page does not establish the two triangle similarities required to conclude the product identity. A complete diagram is not yet a complete proof.

See how to complete the proof
  1. Choose a point X on segment AC such that ∠ABX = ∠DBC. Explain why this is a valid construction.
  2. Use the cyclic angles to show △ABX ∼ △DBC. State corresponding vertices before writing any length ratios.
  3. Then show △XBC ∼ △ABD, again stating correspondence.
  4. From those similarities obtain AX = AB·CD/BD and XC = BC·AD/BD.
    5. AC·BD = AB·CD + BC·AD.

    Because AX + XC = AC, add the two segment identities and multiply by BD. The key habit is to write the triangle correspondence explicitly.

Think about: Which vertices correspond in each similarity, and which equality follows from that correspondence?

NUMBER THEORY 1

Congruences · Benchmark 2

What you established: You organised residue cases for 2, 3 and 5 and obtained the right divisibility conclusion. This is a strong example of using an exhaustive strategy.

What remains: Make the universal step explicit: your residue list covers every integer because each integer belongs to exactly one class modulo each prime.

See how to complete the proof
  1. Write n⁵ − n = n(n−1)(n+1)(n²+1). Three consecutive integers guarantee divisibility by 2 and 3.
  2. For modulo 5, consider n ≡ 0, ±1, ±2. In the first three classes, one of n−1, n, n+1 is divisible by 5.
  3. In the remaining two classes n²+1 ≡ 4+1 ≡ 0 (mod 5).
    4. 2 ∣ (n⁵−n), 3 ∣ (n⁵−n), 5 ∣ (n⁵−n) ⇒ 30 ∣ (n⁵−n).

    Because 2, 3 and 5 are pairwise coprime, their product divides the expression for every integer n.

Think about: Have you explained why your cases cover every possible input?

NUMBER THEORY 2

Greatest common divisors · Benchmark 2

What you established: You found the important fact that a common divisor must divide 2n+1, and you noticed the special residue n ≡ 2 (mod 5).

What remains: Showing 5 can divide both values is not enough. You need to prove that no other prime factor or larger gcd is possible.

See how to complete the proof
  1. Let d = gcd(n²+1, (n+1)²+1). Subtract the two expressions to obtain d ∣ (2n+1).
  2. Take a carefully chosen linear combination of the first number and 2n+1:
  3. Because d divides both terms on the left, it must divide 5. Thus d is either 1 or 5.
  4. d = 5 exactly when n ≡ 2 (mod 5). Show the implication in both directions.
    5. 4(n²+1) − (2n−1)(2n+1) = 5.

    You already had one of the two needed bridges. The linear combination supplies the other: an upper restriction on the gcd.

Think about: When you propose a gcd, have you shown both that it is attained and that nothing larger is possible?

COMBINATORICS 1

Tournaments and induction · Benchmark 2

What you established: You found a promising recursive construction: choose a player, place players who beat that player to the left, and players beaten by that player to the right.

What remains: The written case argument was not completed. Also, one extra claim about a zero-win player implying a zero-loss player is false and should be removed from the proof.

See how to complete the proof
  1. Prove the ordering exists by induction on the number of players. A single player is the base case.
  2. Choose a pivot player P. Split the remaining players into those who beat P and those whom P beats.
  3. Each group is smaller. By induction, order each nonempty group so every player beats the next.
  4. Put them together as: winners of P, then P, then players beaten by P. The junctions are correct because the last winner beats P and P beats the first player on the right.
    5. [players who beat P] → P → [players beaten by P]

    Empty groups simply disappear. This formalises the useful recursive idea you discovered without relying on the false additional claim.

Think about: Does your induction justify the edges between the two smaller groups and the pivot?

COMBINATORICS 2

Pigeonhole principle · Benchmark 2

What you established: Your 3×6 colouring is valid and avoids a monochromatic rectangle. You also identified the correct minimum as seven columns.

What remains: The construction shows that six columns can fail. You also need a proof that every 3×7 colouring must succeed.

See how to complete the proof
  1. In every column, at least two of the three cells have the same colour.
  2. Assign the column one type consisting of the pair of matching rows and that colour.
  3. There are 3 choices of row-pair and 2 choices of colour, making exactly 6 possible types.
  4. With 7 columns, two share a type. Their four cells in the matching rows form a monochromatic rectangle.
    5. 3 row-pairs × 2 colours = 6 types; 7 columns force a repeat.

    Your six-column example proves sharpness. The six-type pigeonhole argument proves that seven always suffice. Together, they establish the minimum.

Think about: In a minimum/maximum problem, have you proved both that the boundary is achievable and that crossing it is impossible?

THE MAIN LESSON

Several of your approaches had a good starting idea. The most useful next question is: what exact statement have I already proved, and what statement is still missing?

When you find candidates

Check them in the original problem for arbitrary inputs.

When you find a boundary

Prove both the construction and the impossibility on the other side.

When you use cases

Explain why the cases are exhaustive.

When you use similarity

Name corresponding vertices before writing ratios.

Try revising just one proof by adding the missing bridge. You do not need to start again from scratch.
